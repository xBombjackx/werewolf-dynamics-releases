Analog gear, simulated from the circuit up. Thirty plugins, free.

Most "analog modeled" plugins are a snapshot: measure the hardware, fit some curves, ship the curves. These solve the actual circuit while your audio runs through it. The tube bias shifts when you hit it hard. The transformer core saturates from flux, so it cares about frequency, not just level. The opto cell remembers what you played ten seconds ago. Nothing is drawn on; if a meter moves or a curve bends, something in the circuit did it.

And since the whole circuit is in there, you get the screwdriver too. Every module has a rear panel with the service trims the hardware kept locked inside: supply draw, bias, transformer headroom, tube swaps. Set them to factory and it behaves like a well-kept unit. Or don't.

VST3 and CLAP on Windows and Linux (beta), Universal VST3 and AU on macOS. No DRM, no account, no nag screens — grab the Windows installer or the zip archives below.

Twenty-two of the plugins run live in your browser (the real C++ DSP compiled to WebAssembly — play the built-in stems or drop in your own WAV file): WD Opto · WD Mu · WD 1B · WD T-Bar · WD FET · WD VCA · WD Duress · WD Tec · WD XQ · WD Pre · WD Tape · WD Echo · WD Brigade · WD Spring · WD AX · WD 361 · WD Span · WD Summer Channel · WD Summer · WD Annihilator · WD Damper · WD Outboard.

Or listen through the 17 A/B audio demos and the full suite overview. Every variant of an A/B demo is the same performance, sample-locked — switching circuits mid-playback is a 12 ms crossfade, so what changes is only the circuit. Loudness-matched, no mastering, no cheating.

The lineup

WD Opto — optical tube leveling compressor. The photocell is the real thing: two time constants, program-dependent memory, that slow settling tail. Two voicings (tube WD-2A, solid-state WD-3A) plus drive and iron controls the original hardware never gave you.

WD Mu — variable-mu compressor built around a simulated remote-cutoff dual-triode push-pull stage and the classic six-position time-constant bank. No threshold knob, because the circuit doesn't have one; you drive it and it leans back. Bias knob on the front, mismatched tube pairs on the back.

WD Tec — passive tube EQ, program and midrange sections in one wide module. The boost you can't get wrong, with a twist: the filter inductors have live magnetic cores, and a Core knob sets how hard you push them.

WD Tape — a full tape machine. Record head, bias oscillator, reproduce head, all of it, aligned by the same procedure a tech would use on the hardware. Four speeds from 30 down to 3.75 ips, three stock formulations, and every unit number ships with its own transport quirks.

WD XQ — one parametric EQ, five circuits. Precision digital, two console flavors, a stepped American classic, and an inductor design from 1973. The knobs stay put while the circuit underneath changes what they're allowed to do. Dynamic bands on all five, plus a Match tool that learns the tonal balance of a reference track.

WD FET — FET compressor with four circuit laws in one box: the classic 76 shunt-FET limiter, a stepped limiter-gate sibling, a germanium path, and a clean precision law. The ratio network is solved from the schematic, so all-buttons works because the circuit does it, not because we faked the curve. Attack is measured in microseconds.

WD VCA — stereo bus compressor built around one current-controlled VCA gain cell. Three detector brains: Console Bus leans the whole mix back the way a classic British desk compressor does, RMS Punch targets the drum bus, and Thrust Bus adds tilted sidechain weighting with switchable feed-forward or feedback sensing. Auto release, soft knee, and the RMS detector's attack table falls out of one capacitor, same as the hardware.

WD Duress — an eight-curve compressor built for abuse. Every ratio position is its own distinct circuit curve: 1:1 is warm-up distortion with no compression at all, 10:1 borrows an opto release with a twenty-second tail, and Nuke is a log-release brickwall. Three distortion modes, two stereo link laws taken from the block diagram, and a British Mode switch that overrides the envelope the way all-buttons abuse does.

WD Clamp — three compressors, one power supply. Load an Opto, Mu, FET, or VCA engine into each of three slots, chain them in any order, and let them share a rail like hardware in a rack does. Drive one slot hard and its neighbors feel it. Switchable -10/-20 dB pad at the input for hot buses, and the transfer screens are measured curves, not drawings, with a dot riding each one at the slot's live input level.

WD Pre — seven preamps behind one gain knob: precision digital wire, a Class A British console with its iron, an American op-amp console, a German broadcast tube amp, a cassette 4-track desk channel, a clean British channel, and a warm triode stage. Gain redistributes the circuit — and keeps pushing into stem overdrive at the top of the dial — while output is only a fader after the iron.

WD Echo — tape loop echo where the loop is real enough to have a splice. Motor-driven transport, three repro heads, spring tank, optional BPM sync. Repeat rate is a motor, not a delay dial: turn it and everything already on the loop bends with the transport. The time arrow reads the tape forward, palindrome, or straight-up backward.

WD AX — the exciter, built from the patents instead of around them. It adds a generated sideband rather than boosting EQ, so the fundamental never moves. The dynamic low-band (Girth) section lifts the decay of a note instead of its hit: sustain without the overload penalty.

WD Rack — the chassis. Put it on your master bus, give your modules the same frame number, and suddenly they're screwed into the same box: shared power supply that sags when a neighbor works hard, adjacent-slot bleed, a slot map you can reorder, and master power and delta switches that flip the whole rack at once. Crank the crosstalk and the rack becomes an effect of its own. Skip it entirely and every module runs clean standalone.

WD Strip — a console channel in one window: preamp into EQ into compressor, a model selector on each stage. Any of the seven Pre laws, either EQ, any of the four compressors. All three stages hang off one power rail inside the same process, so the compressor's knee moves when the preamp leans on the supply. The response curve and compressor transfer on the face are measured live from the running engines, not drawn. No rack required.

WD Summer Channel — the desk channel, one per track. Five consoles on one selector: the British desk with its transformer lows, the big clean inline desk, the discrete-op-amp desk with steel-core lows and a hard fast corner, the EF86 tube console, and a German broadcast amp that stays invisible until you lean on it. Every instance drifts slightly from every other one, keyed to the track name, the way forty channel strips built in 1972 drift from each other.

WD Summer — the bus those channels sum into, and it behaves like one. The more channels you load onto a group, the more the shared supply sags, so a busy mix grabs sooner than a sparse one. That is a mechanism rather than a knob: you cannot turn it up, you can only put more channels on the desk. The same five desks as the channel, bus-sized — with group-wide bypass and delta switches, and loudness-neutralized drive when you push hot stems into the rails.

WD Span — a stereo widener that works with a transformer instead of phase. Everything happens to the difference signal, so a mono fold stays exact no matter how hard you drive it — the thing Haas and allpass wideners cannot claim, because they buy width by spending mono compatibility. Push the iron and the bottom pulls toward the center on its own, because a core saturates on the lows first. No crossover anywhere in it.

WD Spring — a real spring tank, not a spring-flavored algorithm. A steel spring carries high frequencies faster than low ones, so every reflection comes back smeared into a descending chirp. That is the boing, and it is a mechanism rather than a filter, which is why plate algorithms cannot fake it. Three tanks: the short bright pan out of a guitar amp, the studio rack unit, and the long dark console tank.

WD 361 — the tape-side half of a noise reduction unit, used the way engineers misused it. Run the encoder and never decode: quiet material comes up, loud material stays put, and the top comes up hardest — air and density a compressor cannot give you, because a compressor only works on the loud end. ENCODE is the famous trick, DECODE is the opposite tool, and three TYPE families are genuinely different machines. It does not claim to be noise reduction, because the four-band round trip on real program never nulled on the hardware either.

WD Brigade — a bucket-brigade delay, dark for the same reason the originals were. The delay time IS the clock, and a slower clock forces the filters to close down with it: a long BBD delay is necessarily dark, a bright one is necessarily short. Everything people love about these boxes falls out of that one fact — repeats that dissolve into mush, the pitch glide when you grab TIME, the compander hiding the chain's hiss, plus optional BPM sync when you want repeats on the grid. Want the self-oscillation howl? There is a switch on the rear panel, and it ships off.

WD Delta Send & WD Delta Return — a send/return pair that lets you hear what your whole chain is doing: any plugins, anyone's. WD Delta Send taps the untouched signal at the top; WD Delta Return sits at the end, measures the chain's real latency by correlation, and monitors the difference — raw, or level-matched so louder never reads as better. DRY gives you instant whole-chain bypass with no latency jump, WET MATCHED plays the chain at its input's loudness, and LOOK adds lookahead so gain matching stays steady on sudden hits.

Preview Mode (8 plugins)

All eight ship in the main installer and zips and are ready to mix with — four introduced in v0.9.0 (WD Horse Girl, WD Damper, WD Annihilator, and WD VU) and four added in v0.9.1 (WD 1B, WD T-Bar, WD Berth, and WD Outboard). They are marked Preview while we keep dialing in their displays and edge cases toward 1.0.

WD Horse Girl — two-track collision control. Drop one on the bass and one on the kick (or run Mid/Side or Left/Right on one bus), link them on a pair bus, and pick whether they glue together or get out of each other's way across four frequency zones around a movable pivot. Four listener meters above the graph show where the two signals are actually fighting and what the EQ and saturation curves are doing in response.

WD Damper — a resonance suppressor that tells a narrow whistle apart from a wide body boost. Most dynamic notchers eat anything that pokes up; this one measures peak width first so harsh ringing gets pulled down while intentional tone stays put. Runs as smooth time-domain filters or spectral processing.

WD Annihilator — three-band distortion box where Low, Mid, and High each get their own circuit (starved or overfed triodes, sagged diode clippers, negative-feedback iron, calibrated tape hysteresis from clean 0% headroom up to +24 dB ruin, class A, op-amp, or quantum rail) plus Match Bypass autogain. All three bands pull from one shared power supply, so a heavy low end sags the rails for the mids and highs too.

WD VU — ballistic stereo VU and peak meter bridge. Real needle rise and fall times, selectable studio reference levels, and peak hold.

WD 1B — all-tube optical compressor built around a smooth 12 dB soft-knee LED/LDR divider, a push-pull twin-triode gain stage, and a wideband transformer. More open and controllable than fixed-knob opto boxes, with switchable Fixed, Fix/Man, and Manual attack/release timing, continuous 2:1 to 10:1 ratio, sidechain HPF, THD and Iron trims, and WD Rack chassis coupling.

WD T-Bar — mastering variable-mu compressor using matched local-negative-feedback dual-triode stages and wideband iron for low distortion (~0.035% THD) and steady stereo glue. Offers stepped 1.5:1 to 10:1 ratio knees, continuous attack and release (plus dual-stage Auto release), 0–1 ms lookahead inside a fixed 192-sample window, swept sidechain HPF and Tilt, Mid-Side routing, and WD Rack coupling.

WD Berth — zero-latency, FFT-free 24-band dynamic spectral spacer that carves room for a lead vocal, kick, or solo instrument inside a competing bed without FFT pre-ringing. Runs as a Carve receiver or bit-exact Send Key broadcaster over DAW external sidechain, eight internal shared buses (Bus A–H), or WD Outboard's Beacon feed, with Stereo/Mid/Side targeting, Q selectivity, boundary curtains, and SC Iron key conditioning.

WD Outboard — zero-latency mastering converter and analog line-stage simulator paired with a lock-free shared-memory Beacon key broadcaster feeding WD VCA, WD Duress, and WD Berth. Four flavors — Reference Clean (bit-exact digital passthrough at unity), Nickel / Iron (coupled hysteresis and headroom knee), 90s R-2R (ladder nonlinearity and op-amp clipping), and Soft-Clip AD — plus Pre-FX or Post-AD Beacon key tapping and WD Rack coupling.

Requirements

Windows 10 or 11 (64-bit, VST3 or CLAP), macOS 10.15 Catalina and newer on Intel or Apple Silicon (Universal VST3 or AU), or Linux x86_64 (VST3 or CLAP beta; requires libwebkit2gtk-4.1-0 for the UI).

Install

Windows: run WerewolfDynamics-v0.9.1-Windows-Installer.exe, or unzip WerewolfDynamics-v0.9.1-Windows-VST3-CLAP.zip below and copy the .vst3 folders into C:\Program Files\Common Files\VST3 or the .clap files into C:\Program Files\Common Files\CLAP (per-user alternative: %LOCALAPPDATA%\Programs\Common\CLAP). Pick one format per plugin, then rescan in your DAW — everything shows up under Werewolf Dynamics.

macOS: unzip WerewolfDynamics-v0.9.1-macOS-VST3-AU.zip below (or from GitHub Releases) and copy the .vst3 bundles into /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/VST3 or the .component bundles into /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/Components. The builds are not notarized by Apple yet, so macOS will quarantine them on first run — INSTALL.txt inside the zip walks through clearing it in one command.

Linux (beta): download the Linux tarball below or from GitHub Releases and extract into ~/.vst3 or ~/.clap.

Windows and macOS builds are not code-signed yet, so SmartScreen or Gatekeeper may grumble on first run. Rescan once after updating so your DAW picks up the updated sidechain routing and parameters.
